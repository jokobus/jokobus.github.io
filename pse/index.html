Interactive Periodic Table of the Elements

A comprehensive, bilingual, and interactive Periodic Table of the Elements (PSE) featuring all 118 chemical elements, full physical and chemical properties, Steckbrief popups, and collapsible transition metal & f-block views.

📅 17. Oct 2026 ⏱️ ~3 min read (567 words) #Chemistry #Science #Periodic Table #Interactive #Engineering

Welcome to the Interactive Periodic Table of the Elements (Periodensystem der Elemente). Designed for scientists, engineers, students, and educators, this table brings all 118 chemical elements to life with high information density, responsive layouts, and seamless light/dark mode integration.

💡
Interactive Controls
  • Click any element to open its detailed Steckbrief modal, including electron configurations, oxidation states, melting/boiling points, density, discovery details, and official Wikipedia links.
  • Toggle Transition Metals (3–12) to seamlessly collapse the table into the classic 8 Main Groups (I to VIII) layout.
  • Toggle Lanthanoids & Actinoids to expand or collapse the f-block.
  • Instant Search: Type element symbols (e.g. Fe), names (Iron), or atomic numbers (26) in the search bar.
  • Direct Link: Use URL hashes like #/pse/#el-Au or #/pse/#26 to jump straight to any element!

Reading the Element Tile

Each tile is packed with physical and chemical constants, matching the layout of standard scientific reference charts:

┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  1.008 (Atomic Mass Ar)         2.20 (EN)     │
│                                               │
│  1 (Atomic Number Z)            H (Symbol)    │
│                                               │
│  -259° / -253° (mp/bp)   Hydrogen   0.09 g/L  │
└───────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
  1. Top-Left: Relative atomic mass ($A_r$), based on standard atomic weights (IUPAC). For synthetic radioactive elements without stable isotopes, the mass number of the longest-lived isotope is bracketed.
  2. Top-Right: Electronegativity according to the Pauling scale.
  3. Center-Left: Atomic number ($Z$), representing nuclear proton count.
  4. Center: Chemical symbol, styled and color-coded by its thermodynamic state of matter at $20^\circ\text{C}$ (solid, liquid, gas, or synthetic).
  5. Bottom-Left: Melting point and boiling point in degrees Celsius ($^\circ\text{C}$).
  6. Bottom-Center: Element name in English (or German when toggled).
  7. Bottom-Right: Density at $20^\circ\text{C}$ ($1\,\text{atm}$).

Key Periodic Trends

The Periodic Table arranges elements by atomic number $Z$ and recurrent valence electron configurations. This gives rise to predictable physical and chemical trends:

1. Electronegativity

Electronegativity ($\chi$) quantifies an atom’s ability to attract shared electron pairs in covalent chemical bonds:

  • Across a Period (left to right): $\chi$ increases systematically due to increasing effective nuclear charge ($Z_{\text{eff}}$) pulling valence electrons tighter without adding new shielding shells.
  • Down a Group (top to bottom): $\chi$ decreases as valence electrons occupy shells further from the nucleus, shielded by inner electron cores.
  • Extremes: Cesium ($\chi \approx 0.79$) and Francium ($\chi \approx 0.7$) are the least electronegative metals, while Fluorine ($\chi = 3.98$) is the most electronegative element.

2. Atomic Radius & Lanthanide Contraction

The spatial extent of electron probability distributions defines atomic radius:

  • Atomic radii decrease from left to right across periods due to stronger Coulomb attraction from the unshielded nuclear charge.
  • Atomic radii increase down groups as principal quantum number $n$ increases.
  • In Period 6, the filling of the $4f$ subshell between Lanthanum ($Z=57$) and Lutetium ($Z=71$) causes the famous Lanthanide Contraction. Because $f$-electrons provide poor electrostatic shielding, elements following the lanthanides (e.g. Hafnium, Tantalum, Gold) exhibit nearly identical atomic radii to their lighter Period 5 congeners (Zirconium, Niobium, Silver), making their chemical separation exceptionally challenging.

3. Transition Metals & Main Groups View

In introductory chemistry and stoichiometry, understanding valence electrons is simplified by focusing solely on the s- and p-block elements:

  • The 8 Main Groups (I A through VIII A) correspond to valence electron numbers from 1 to 8, directly determining Lewis octet structures, ionic charges (e.g. $\text{Na}^+$, $\text{Ca}^{2+}$, $\text{Cl}^-$, $\text{O}^{2-}$), and bonding valencies.
  • By toggling the Transition Metals (3–12) button above, you can collapse the d-block to isolate these fundamental relationships.
